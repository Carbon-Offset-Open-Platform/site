Carbon reporting today is a once-a-year estimate. It should work like an ERP module.

SCOOP measures emissions and removals as events: a specific asset, a specific interval, a specific owner. Every figure carries its method, provenance, uncertainty and assurance, so it can be audited, settled and reconciled across industries.

Observation integrity

Every measurement starts somewhere real.

From a smart power strip on a lab server to a satellite reading the atmosphere, SCOOP keeps the link between a reported tonne and the instrument that observed it. Independent sources, like NASA's Orbiting Carbon Observatory 2, are how higher maturity levels reconcile what companies report.

Seven principles

Built to the principles behind P7802.

01

Interoperability

Emissions and removals reconcile across industries like debits and credits.

02

Measuring externalities

Scope 3 enters the books through crediting and debiting, mirroring cost accounting.

03

Verifiability

Recompute any published figure from stored readings and get the same answer.

04

Decentralization

No single authority. Sources of truth work the way DNS and IANA do.

05

Privacy

Evidence crosses trust boundaries. Raw data, customer and workload identities never do.

06

Scalability

One core model, adapted to any sector's projects, many live data streams.

07

Traceability

Auditable lineage from every reported quantity back to its source evidence.

Each principle ships with a test that proves it.

How it works

From the meter to the registry, without losing the thread.

SCOOP sits in the middle of the carbon supply chain. Projects feed it observations; it ties each one to the right asset, owner and interval, then hands verifiable evidence up to accounting systems, exchanges and registries.

The liability journey

Follow one carbon event from the plug to the books.

  1. 01
    Measure

    A smart power strip samples the server about every six seconds.

  2. 02
    Create the event

    Power over an interval (t1, t2) becomes kWh, then kg CO₂e with the grid factor. A unique token, still to be built, will stop it being counted twice.

  3. 03
    Transfer

    The liability passes to the tenant whose workload drew the power.

  4. 04
    Offset

    The tenant can retire carbon credits against it…

  5. 05
    Discharge

    …or pay a fee to discharge it…

  6. 06
    Geological carbon equity

    …and if nobody takes it, it falls to the geological carbon equity account. Nothing vanishes from the books.

IEEE P7802

We don't just implement the standard. We convene it.

P7802 defines a core model that harmonizes industry MMRV methods rather than replacing them. SCOOP convenes the working group, and its code is meant to be the open reference implementation. Meetings are open to anyone.

Projects

Start from the problem you already have.

Carbon data today is inconsistent, illiquid, incompatible, opaque and late. Each SCOOP project takes on one of those problems for a real business or policy team, and every project runs on the same open core.

Too slow · Opaque

Data center monitor

“We can’t see what our compute is emitting until the quarter is over.”

Shows energy use and carbon emissions at the market interval, from power readings taken every few seconds, so data center managers can judge how AI code efficiency affects emissions and what their renewables actually deliver.

Working prototypeSCOOP Lab, Oregon
Illiquid

Dynamic, high-liquidity carbon exchange

“Carbon can’t settle at the speed the rest of the market moves.”

Explores settling carbon trades at the market interval, simulating digital carbon assets and showing how energy efficiency and data accuracy are rewarded. SCOOP supplies the standard and the apps; operational-scale trading would run on partners’ rails.

Early conversationsEmitters and financial actors
Inconsistent · Opaque

Agroforestry claims portal

“Forest carbon claims are hard to compare, audit or act on without a middleman.”

A portal for specifying, storing and analyzing carbon claims, with what-if analysis for harvest and management decisions. It lets forest owners speak directly to emitters and could speed up auditing by rating agencies. Also planned for the Sabalito Nature Reserve in Costa Rica.

ScopingCredit side · removals
Opaque · Incompatible

Community impact of data centers

“We need to understand what a proposed data center means for our land and energy.”

Reuses the data center monitor and claims portal so Indigenous communities in Australia can analyze data center impacts, from generation and water to transmission, and bring that data into their own business planning.

ScopingBuilds on the monitor and portal

    Our role

    A utility, not an operator.

    Think of SCOOP as the app store, not the rails. We set the open standard and build the reusable apps and prototypes. Partners run the operational-grade, secure, high-volume systems on top of them.

    Open protocol

    Ask any number where it came from.

    A self-service API for developers, providers, policy makers and auditors. A read-only v0 is live now with the data center prototype’s measurements, so you can check every figure against its samples. Schemas in JSON Schema with JSON-LD context, exportable to RDF, are planned. Copyleft and developed in the open.

    About

    The people behind SCOOP.

    SCOOP is housed in the Tufts Auster Center's Environmental Innovation Initiative, which brings together researchers, citizens and managers with expertise in sustainability science, data science and financial accounting to make environmental measurement more transparent, credible and inclusive.

    Katrina Pugh, Ph.D., MS/MBA

    About the Faculty Fellow

    Katrina (Kate) Pugh is Auster Center’s Environmental Innovation Faculty Fellow and teaches at Tufts Gordon Institute's MS in Engineering Management. Dr. Pugh is a consultant, researcher, and educator focused on strategy, networks, and sustainability. Dr. Pugh leverages AI to analyze social systems — such as how conversation features influence outcomes. She employs systems thinking and quantitative modeling to improve knowledge sharing, open source, impact networks, and project performance. She co-leads sustainability conversation skills-building through the Conversation for Civility network and teaches changemakers on impact networks as a Partner with Weaving Futures. She has been published in various journals, including KM for Development Journal, MIT Sloan Management Review, and Harvard Business Review. She is the author of Sharing Hidden Know-How. Dr. Pugh is an Adjunct Professor for Columbia University’s Information and Knowledge Strategy master’s program, where she also served as Academic Director. She earned a Bachelor of Arts degree in economics from Williams College, a Master of Science degree and an MBA from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and a PhD in ecology and environmental sciences from the University of Maine.

    Enrique Castro-Leon, Ph.D.

    About the Sponsor and Engineering Advisor

    Enrique Castro-Leon is Founder and Executive Director of the SCOOP Carbon Accounting Project, a volunteer-driven, open-source initiative developing cross-industry information technology frameworks for environmental accounting, beginning with greenhouse gas accounting. The project's objectives are to advance environmental accounting toward a level of rigor comparable to financial accounting and to facilitate the acquisition, exchange, and assurance of environmental data across supply chains and organizational boundaries. SCOOP research and development activities will be conducted under the leadership of the Auster SCOOP Environmental Innovation Initiative, where he serves as a member of the Board of Advisors.

    Dr. Castro-Leon is also Chair of the IEEE P7802 Standards Working Group, which is developing standards for measurement, monitoring, reporting, and verification (MMRV) in climate action projects. In addition, he is Chief Technology Officer and a founding member of OptimiLabs, a startup focused on systems integration and technology innovation. His professional experience includes leadership roles at Intel, where he helped drive the transformation of traditional engineering laboratories into cloud-based Lab-as-a-Service environments. He also led initiatives to integrate power monitoring and control capabilities into data center solutions and, as an enterprise architecture consultant, advised senior executives on technology strategy, integration, and transformation.

    His academic experience includes appointments as Adjunct Professor at Portland State University, the Oregon Graduate Institute, and the University of Costa Rica. Dr. Castro-Leon has authored or co-authored four books, contributed to IEEE publications and peer-reviewed research, and is an inventor on three patents.

    He holds a B.S. in Electrical Engineering from the University of Costa Rica, and both an M.S. in Electrical Engineering and Computer Science and a Ph.D. in Electrical Engineering from Purdue University.
